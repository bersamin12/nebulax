# Finder 3 — fatigue damage estimation from stress time series (ML + spectral methods)

Sweep for the PS3 addendum. Problem restated: 64 labelled + 16 test files, **one** dynamic-stress
channel of 581,120 samples (sample rate unstated), target = cumulative fatigue damage per file
(0.03–0.93, bimodal), organiser label generated by rainflow + Palmgren–Miner + an S-N curve, metric
`1 − MAPE`. Observed: a plain rainflow+Miner fit on the given channel does **not** reproduce the
labels (LOO MAPE ≥ 0.78 for any exponent), while peak-to-peak amplitude statistics correlate at
Spearman 0.96.

Nothing on fatigue, rainflow, Miner, S-N curves or spectral damage exists in
`docs/research/model_ladder.md`, `rail_phm.md`, `datasets.md` or `references.md` — I checked
(`grep -i "fatigue|rainflow|miner|dirlik|s-n|damage|spectral moment|EN 12663|carbody|bogie frame"`);
the only hits are bearing-degradation uses of the word "damage". So this whole area is new to the
team and there is no duplication to avoid. The one connection worth making is **V1 in
`model_ladder.md` ("simple beats deep")** — the fatigue literature says the same thing here, and
more strongly, for the reason given in §B below.

## A. The single most important physical fact for this problem

For a stationary Gaussian narrow-band process, the closed-form Palmgren–Miner damage intensity
(Bendat, reproduced as Eq. (14) of the FLife review [F1]) is

```
D = (ν0+ · T / C) · (√2 · σ)^k · Γ(1 + k/2)
```

where `σ² = m0` is the signal variance, `ν0+` the zero-upcrossing rate from the spectral moments,
and `k` the S-N exponent. **Damage is a power law in the amplitude scale.** Consequently:

* `log D` is *affine* in `log σ` (or in `log`(any other amplitude scale statistic: RMS,
  peak-to-peak, p99−p01 range, rainflow-range percentile). A Spearman of 0.96 against peak-to-peak
  is exactly what this predicts and is **not** a sign that the labels are trivial — it is the
  physics. Ranking well ≠ hitting MAPE, because the residual factor `ν0+ · Γ(1+k/2)` and the
  broad-band correction still move damage by tens of percent.
* The *right* model class is therefore **linear-in-log-space regression of `log D` on `log` of a
  handful of amplitude and spectral-shape features**, not a deep net on 581k raw samples. See the
  practitioner-defaults paragraph.
* `1 − MAPE` is a **relative**-error metric, so fitting `log D` with ordinary least squares is
  nearly the right loss (OLS in log space ≈ minimising relative error), and much better conditioned
  than fitting `D` directly across a 30× dynamic range with a bimodal label distribution.

## B. Why a plain rainflow+Miner fit can legitimately fail to reproduce a rainflow+Miner label

This is the team's actual blocker, so I searched it hardest. Six published mechanisms, each of which
changes the damage number **without changing the signal**, ranked by how much I'd bet on it:

1. **Rainflow residue handling.** After the rainflow algorithm closes all hysteresis loops, a
   *residue* of unclosed half-cycles remains. There is no single convention: discard, count as half
   cycles, count as full cycles, or "repeat-history" closure. [F4] reviews these and shows the
   choice materially changes the damage sum, and that the common conventions are non-conservative.
   Two standard Python packages disagree here (`fatpack` is 4-point, `rainflow` is 3-point
   ASTM E1049) — **check both before concluding anything**. Cheapest, highest-yield experiment.
2. **The label may be computed on a *transformed* channel, not the raw one.** Standard practice in
   vibration qualification is to push the measured signal through an SDOF transfer function (or a
   bank of them) before counting — this is the Fatigue Damage Spectrum construction [F16][F17]. If
   the organisers did that, raw-channel rainflow will never match at any exponent, but FDS features
   (damage at a handful of octave-spaced natural frequencies, Q≈10) will. This exactly matches the
   symptom "no exponent works, but the amplitude scale is right".
3. **A knee / cut-off in the S-N curve.** Whether cycles below the fatigue limit are dropped
   (original Miner), kept on the same slope (elementary Miner), or kept on a shallower slope
   `k* = 2k−1` (Haibach) changes which part of the amplitude distribution dominates. With 581k
   samples per file the small-amplitude tail is huge, so this is a large lever. `UNVERIFIED`
   primary source for Haibach — I have the rule from secondary sources only; the FLife review [F1]
   and [F13] both use single-slope curves.
4. **Mean-stress correction** (Goodman / Morrow / Smith–Watson–Topper) applied by the organisers and
   not by you. Rainflow retains the cycle mean; spectral methods discard it, and [F1] §4.2
   explicitly justifies discarding it (rainflow cycle means are t-distributed about zero). If the
   label used Goodman and your fit does not, you get a systematic, amplitude-dependent bias.
5. **The label is a hot-spot / different-location damage.** Standard SHM practice is to measure at
   an accessible point and transfer to the critical point through an FE-derived or modal transfer
   function [F10][F11]; the damage at the gauge is *not* the damage at the notch. If the organisers
   simulated the structure and reported damage at a critical node, the single published channel is a
   proxy and the mapping is a (possibly frequency-dependent) gain.
6. **Irreducible realisation scatter.** [F5] quantifies the variability of Miner damage caused
   purely by the randomness of one realisation of a stationary load — damage from a single
   time-history is one draw from a distribution. [F1] measured this concretely: RFC damage from a
   fragment converged to within 2% of the 1-hour value only after ~2 s for steel (k=3.3), ~150 s for
   aluminium (k=7.3) and ~2600 s for spring steel (k=11.8). **The higher the exponent, the longer
   the record you need.** If the exponent is high and 581,120 samples is a short record, there is a
   noise floor on `1 − MAPE` that no model beats. Worth estimating early by bootstrapping damage
   over sub-windows of one file.

Also worth knowing: a free multiplicative constant (unknown `C`, unknown `T` because the sample rate
is unstated) is *harmless* — fit it. Since fitting it still fails, the failure is in the **shape** of
the damage-vs-file relation, not its scale, which is what points at mechanisms 1–5 rather than a
units problem.

## D. Practitioner defaults — what a rail/PHM engineer builds first with ~12 h

Spend the first 90 minutes **not modelling**. Run the three rainflow implementations (`fatpack`
4-point, `rainflow` 3-point, `FLife`'s wrapper) over one file with every residue convention and a
grid of `k`, and plot the resulting damage against the label for all 64 files on log–log axes. That
single plot separates the hypotheses in §B: parallel-but-offset means a scale/`C`/`T` problem (fix
by fitting the constant); different slope means the wrong `k`; scatter that correlates with the
signal's *bandwidth* (irregularity factor α₂ from spectral moments) means the label came through an
SDOF/FDS transform or a broad-band correction; scatter that correlates with *kurtosis* means a
non-Gaussian correction [F18]. Then build the boring model, which is what will actually score:
compute ~30 cheap features per file — RMS (√m0), peak-to-peak, p99−p01 and a few rainflow-range
percentiles, kurtosis and skewness, spectral moments m0/m1/m2/m4 with ν0⁺, νp and the irregularity
and Vanmarcke bandwidth parameters, FLife's Dirlik / Tovo–Benasciutti / narrow-band damage at three
or four candidate `k` values, and an 8–12 band FDS (SDOF filter bank, Q=10, octave-spaced, rainflow
per band) — then **regress `log(damage)` on `log(features)` with ridge or a short-length-scale GP,
leave-one-out over the 64 files**, and report `1 − MAPE` in the original space. Fitting in log space
is the right thing for a relative-error metric *and* matches the physics (§A); with n=64 anything
deeper than gradient boosting on 30 features will overfit, which is the same verdict the team
already reached in `model_ladder.md` V1 and which the wind-DEL literature independently confirms —
[F8] gets R²=0.988 on blade-root DEL from **three scalars**, and their temporal-convolution network
on the full time series adds only ~0.003. Keep a physics baseline in the leaderboard (best spectral
method with fitted `C` and `k`) so the ML row has something honest to beat, and bound the ceiling
early by bootstrapping damage over sub-windows of a single file [F1][F5] — if the realisation
scatter is, say, 8%, then `1 − MAPE = 0.92` is a win and chasing 0.98 is chasing noise. The two
things I would *not* do in 12 h: a 1D-CNN/LSTM on 581k raw samples with 64 training examples, and
any multiaxial or critical-plane machinery — there is one channel, so there is no second channel to
be multiaxial with.

* **MUST** — F1/FLife spectral sweep (2 h); F4 residue-convention experiment (1 h); log-space
  feature regression per §D (4 h); F5/F1 realisation-scatter noise floor (1 h).
* **NICE** — F16 FDS features (3 h, the single most likely fix if the label is SDOF-transformed);
  F18 kurtosis correction (1 h); F8-style small NN as an ablation row (3 h); F14 damage-consistency
  calibration framing for the write-up (2 h).
* **SKIP, with reason** — deep sequence models on raw samples (n=64; [F8] shows the time-series
  network buys ~0.3% over three scalars); multiaxial / critical-plane criteria (one channel);
  rainflow-matrix extrapolation F9 (we are not extrapolating to a longer life, the record *is* the
  label's domain); `ffpack` (GPL-3.0 in a shipped deliverable).
